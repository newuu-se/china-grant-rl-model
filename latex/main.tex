\begin{abstract}
We study energy-efficient driving of an ER9E electric multiple unit on the
74.9\,km Toshkent--Ho'jakent line using deep reinforcement learning (RL)
coupled, step by step, to a modified version of the NeTrainSim train
dynamics simulator. The simulator is adapted to regional (GOST-based)
resistance formulas, regenerative braking is disabled, and an interactive
JSON bridge gives the learning agent true per-second control of the
locomotive throttle notch. A Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent
selects one of nine notch settings every 15 simulated seconds to minimize
trip energy subject to a 6{,}500\,s schedule deadline. We report the
complete diagnosis chain required to make this work: (i)~elevation noise in
the route data produced physically impossible grades of up to $\pm17\%$ that
broke the simulator's force balance and corrupted its energy accounting,
which we repair with an elevation-preserving median filter; (ii)~stale
observation normalization silently clipped the gradient and power features
the agent needed; (iii)~a schedule-dependent reward without a clock in the
observation made the reward non-Markovian; and (iv)~a constant entropy bonus
kept the policy pinned at the uniform distribution, which we resolve with
linear entropy annealing. With all repairs in place, the learned stochastic
policy attains \emph{on-schedule parity with the constant-notch Pareto
frontier} (790.0\kwh{} in 6{,}417\,s, within $\sim$1\kwh{} of the
interpolated frontier), uses 6.9\% less energy than the simulator's native
driver, and 8.4\% less than flat-out driving. On the descending return trip
the agent finds very low-energy but slightly late solutions (169.6\kwh,
8.9\% late), exposing a remaining reward-calibration issue: lateness priced
too cheaply causes the policy mode to settle one notch below the
schedule-feasible optimum. We characterize both trips' energy--time
frontiers, quantify every failure mode, and outline the calibration fix.
The study doubles as a practical checklist for simulator-in-the-loop RL on
long-horizon control problems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

Railway traction is one of the largest electricity consumers in many
national grids, and the driving style of a train over a fixed route with a
fixed timetable is a classical lever for reducing that consumption: between
the fastest feasible trip and the slowest schedule-feasible one lies an
energy--time trade-off that a skilled driver (or an automatic train
operation system) can exploit~\cite{scheepmaker2017,howlett1995,yin2017}.
Classical approaches derive optimal driving regimes (maximum acceleration,
cruising, coasting, braking) from optimal-control theory. Deep
reinforcement learning offers a complementary, model-free route: if a
high-fidelity simulator of the train and route exists, an agent can learn
the energy-optimal throttle policy directly from interaction, without
hand-deriving switching points, and can in principle adapt to terrain,
speed-limit structure, and schedule slack simultaneously.

This paper reports a complete, honest account of building such a system for
a concrete regional case: the Toshkent--Ho'jakent line in Uzbekistan
(74.9\,km, 40--80\,km/h speed zones, net climb of 347\,m), operated by an
ER9E electric multiple unit. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A simulator-in-the-loop RL pipeline.} We extend the
  open-source NeTrainSim freight simulator~\cite{aredah2024} with
  (a)~GOST-based resistance physics appropriate to the region,
  (b)~removal of regenerative braking to match the studied rolling stock,
  and (c)~an interactive stdin/stdout JSON protocol that lets a Gymnasium
  environment drive the C++ physics one second at a time, bypassing the
  simulator's built-in driver and optimizer.

  \item \textbf{Route-data repair.} The digital elevation model behind the
  route data contained single-node errors producing grades up to $\pm17\%$
  on 50\,m segments---physically impossible for rail, sufficient to break
  the simulator's force balance, and inflating its energy accounting by a
  factor of three at the affected links. We repair the profile with an
  elevation-preserving median filter and re-measure all baselines on the
  cleaned data (Section~\ref{sec:data}).

  \item \textbf{A reproducible diagnosis chain for long-horizon RL.} We
  document four distinct failure modes encountered on the way to a working
  agent---observation saturation, a non-Markovian schedule reward, an
  exploration--commitment deadlock under a constant entropy bonus, and
  lateness underpricing---together with the measurements that exposed each
  one (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

  \item \textbf{Quantified results on both directions of the line.} The
  final forward-trip policy reaches the constant-notch energy--time Pareto
  frontier \emph{on schedule} (790.0\kwh{} / 6{,}417\,s), beating the
  simulator's native driver by 6.9\% and flat-out driving by 8.4\%. We
  further characterize the descending return trip, where the same recipe
  converges to very low energy (169.6\kwh) at the cost of an 8.9\% deadline
  violation---isolating reward calibration, not optimization, as the last
  open problem (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

We deliberately report what the agent does \emph{not} yet achieve: it
matches, but does not move below, the constant-notch frontier; and its
greedy (argmax) mode systematically settles one notch below the
schedule-feasible optimum. Both observations come with a quantitative
explanation and a concrete fix.

\section{System and Methods}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Simulator and physics modifications}
\label{sec:simulator}

NeTrainSim~\cite{aredah2024} is an open-source C++/Qt network train
simulator that integrates longitudinal train dynamics (tractive effort
vs.\ resistance, adhesion-limited) at a configurable time step, here
1\,s. We modified it in three ways.

\paragraph{GOST-based resistance.} The original Davis-type resistance in
US units is replaced by the SI specific-resistance form used in regional
(GOST) railway practice. For a vehicle of mass $m$ tonnes at speed $v$
km/h, the running resistance in newtons is
\begin{equation}
R \;=\; \underbrace{\bigl(1.1 + 0.01\,v + 0.000227\,v^2\bigr)}_{w_0\ \mathrm{[N/kN]}}\, W
\;+\; \underbrace{10\, i_{\%}}_{\mathrm{grade\ [N/kN]}}\, W
\;+\; \underbrace{\frac{700}{1746.4}\,|c^{\circ}|}_{\mathrm{curve\ [N/kN]}}\, W,
\qquad W = 9.81\, m \ \mathrm{[kN]},
\label{eq:resistance}
\end{equation}
where $i_{\%}$ is the gradient in percent (the factor 10 converts to
per-mille, the unit of the GOST grade term) and the curve term implements
$w_r = 700/R_c$ with curve radius $R_c = 1746.4/c^{\circ}$ derived from the
curvature in degrees of arc.\footnote{The unit of the curvature column in
the source data could not be independently confirmed; with the recorded
maximum of $0.54^{\circ}$ the implied minimum radius is $\sim$3.2\,km and
the curve term is everywhere small. \todo{confirm curvature unit with the
data provider.}}

\paragraph{No regenerative braking.} The studied configuration does not
recover braking energy; the simulator's regeneration path is disabled
(regenerative efficiency $\equiv 0$), making braking purely dissipative.

\paragraph{Interactive RL bridge.} In interactive mode
(\texttt{-I}), the binary executes a loop: read a JSON action
$\{\texttt{notch}\,{:}\,u\}$ from stdin $\rightarrow$ override every
locomotive's throttle with the quadratic notch map
$\lambda = (u/8)^2$ $\rightarrow$ advance the physics one second
$\rightarrow$ write the train state (speed, position, directional grade and
curvature at the head, per-step energy, link speed limit, arrival flag) as
one JSON line to stdout. The override bypasses both the built-in A$^*$
speed optimizer and the discretized throttle governor, so the agent's
action is the throttle physically applied. One subtlety matters for
bidirectional use: each link stores both directional gradients
$\{+i, -i\}$ and the physics resolves the correct sign from the traversal
order of the train's path; the state emission must use the same
direction-aware lookup, otherwise observations on a reversed path carry
inverted gradient signs while the physics remains correct.

Table~\ref{tab:setup} summarizes the configuration. The route's grade
column, derived from a digital elevation model, contained isolated
single-node errors with a characteristic signature: adjacent
opposite-sign grade pairs of up to $-12.4\%$/$+17.0\%$ on consecutive 50\,m
segments (e.g.\ links 887/888). Real rail grades rarely exceed 3--4\%.
These spikes are not merely cosmetic. At $+17\%$ the grade resistance of
the 373\,t consist ($\approx$620\,kN) exceeds the train's total adhesion
limit ($\approx$441\,kN), the simulator's smoothed acceleration no longer
satisfies force balance, and its energy accounting---which charges the
\emph{virtual} tractive power $(m a + R)\,v$---records up to
3.76\kwh{} in a single second, an implied 11.8\,MW from a consist with
3.64\,MW installed.

We repair the data in elevation space: integrate the grades into an
elevation profile, apply three passes of a window-3 median filter (which
removes isolated spikes exactly while leaving monotone slopes untouched)
plus one light moving-average pass on interior nodes with both endpoints
pinned, and re-derive the grades. The net climb is preserved bit-for-bit
(346.619\,m); the grade range contracts from $[-12.4, +17.0]\%$ to
$[-2.7, +4.5]\%$; the per-second energy maximum falls from 3.76 to
1.39\kwh{} (a notch-8 acceleration transient, within installed power).
Figure~\ref{fig:cleaning} shows the raw and repaired profiles. All
baselines and results below use the repaired data.

\subsection{Markov decision process formulation}
\label{sec:mdp}

\paragraph{Observation (9 features, all normalized).} Speed
$v/22.22$, position $x/L$, directional grade $i/4.5$, curvature,
remaining distance $(L-x)/L$, per-step energy $E_t/1.5$, link speed limit
$v_{\lim}/22.22$, elapsed-time fraction $\tau = t/T_d$, and
\emph{behind-schedule fraction}
\begin{equation}
\beta_t \;=\; \max\!\Bigl(0,\ \tfrac{t}{T_d}L - x_t\Bigr)\big/ L .
\end{equation}
The last two features are essential, not cosmetic: the reward below depends
on the step counter, so an agent without a clock in its observation faces a
non-Markovian reward it cannot learn to predict
(Section~\ref{sec:results}). Equally essential are the normalization
denominators: earlier values calibrated to a previous dataset clipped the
grade feature to a sign bit on 27\% of the route and saturated the energy
feature at 17\% of its true range---the agent was blind to precisely the
terrain and power information that terrain-aware driving requires.

\paragraph{Action.} One of nine notch settings, held for 15 consecutive
simulator seconds (action repeat). The decision horizon of an episode drops
from $\sim$6{,}500 one-second choices to $\sim$430, which we found necessary
for credit assignment over the trip-length energy sum; with one-second
decisions every reward design we tried collapsed to a near-constant notch.

\paragraph{Reward.} Per simulated second $t$ (summed over the 15\,s
decision), with one smoothness term per decision:
\begin{equation}
r_t \;=\; -\,w_E\, E_t \;-\; w_P\, \beta_t \;-\; w_V \max(0,\, v_t - v_{\lim})
\;\;\underbrace{-\; w_S\, |\Delta u|}_{\text{per decision}}
\;+\;
\begin{cases}
+B_{\mathrm{arr}} & \text{on arrival},\\[2pt]
-B_{\mathrm{to}} & \text{on timeout},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
with $w_E{=}3$ per kWh, $w_P{=}2$, $w_V{=}1$, $w_S{=}0.15$,
$B_{\mathrm{arr}}{=}200$, $B_{\mathrm{to}}{=}1500$ (timeout at 12{,}000\,s).
The pace penalty $w_P \beta_t$ is zero whenever the train is on or ahead of
the uniform deadline pace, so coasting is free where slack exists and
lagging is penalized immediately; a terminal-only lateness fee is provably
ineffective here because, over a $\sim$6{,}000-step episode, an immediate
per-step energy saving dominates any discounted end-of-trip penalty.

\paragraph{Why near-undiscounted.} The objective is a $\sim$6{,}000-step
energy \emph{sum}; with $\gamma \le 0.999$ the discounting itself distorts
the sum toward ``finish sooner'' (high notch). We use $\gamma = 0.9999$
(effective horizon $\sim$10$^4$ steps) with return normalization for value
stability.

Table~\ref{tab:hyper} lists the final configuration. One element deserves
emphasis: \textbf{entropy annealing}. With a constant entropy coefficient
(0.004), the policy never left the uniform distribution over 100 epochs
(policy entropy $\approx \ln 9 = 2.197$ throughout; flat action histogram;
the argmax parked at notch~0 and timed out): the entropy gradient exceeded
the tiny per-decision advantage gradient that a 15-second notch choice
exerts on a $\sim$2{,}500-unit episode return. Annealing the coefficient
linearly to zero lets early exploration give way to commitment; entropy
then falls (to 1.24 forward, 0.33 return at 200 epochs) and test rewards
improve monotonically (Figure~\ref{fig:training}).

Table~\ref{tab:frontier} gives the energy--time Pareto frontiers obtained
by driving each constant notch to completion. Three observations frame
everything that follows. First, the frontier is shallow at the top and
steep at the bottom: between notch 8 and notch 4 the forward trip saves

comparable time): varied driving is not automatically better driving.

\subsection{The diagnosis chain}

Table~\ref{tab:runs} summarizes the training runs. Three failure modes had
to be identified and removed before any meaningful learning occurred; each
is documented here because the symptoms are generic to long-horizon,
simulator-in-the-loop RL.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\caption{Training-run summary (all on repaired data, 9-feature
observation). ``Sampled'' rollouts draw from the policy distribution (the
behavior the test rewards measure); ``argmax'' is the deterministic mode.}
\label{tab:runs}
\begin{tabular}{llllll}
\toprule
Run & Trip & Best reward & Sampled rollout & Argmax rollout & Diagnosis \\
\midrule
A (100 ep, const.\ ent.) & fwd & $-2470$ & uniform histogram & N0, timeout & entropy pinned at $\ln 9$ \\
B (100 ep, annealed) & fwd & $-2323$ & 800.2\kwh, 6{,}708\,s & const.\ N2, 31\% late & sharp but late \\
C (200 ep, annealed) & fwd & $-2254$ & \textbf{790.0\kwh, 6{,}417\,s} & const.\ N1, timeout & \textbf{frontier parity} \\
\midrule
D (100 ep, annealed) & ret & $-906$ & 319.7\kwh, 5{,}827\,s & const.\ N4, on time & still descending \\
E (200 ep, annealed) & ret & $-620$ & 169.6\kwh, 7{,}076\,s & const.\ N1, 18\% late & overshot deadline \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{(i) Observation saturation.} Before this study's repairs, the
grade feature was normalized by a stale constant ($0.7\%$) appropriate to an
earlier dataset. With real grades up to $\pm4.5\%$ (clean) the feature
clipped to $\pm1$ on 27\% of the route: a 1\% and a 4\% climb were
indistinguishable. The per-step energy feature similarly saturated at
0.25\kwh{} against a true maximum of 1.39\kwh. No terrain-aware policy is
learnable when the policy cannot see terrain magnitude; conclusions drawn
about RL's limits before such audits are unreliable.

\paragraph{(ii) Non-Markovian reward.} The pace penalty in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward} depends on the elapsed step count, which was
originally absent from the observation. The critic then faces targets it
cannot predict from its inputs, and the policy cannot condition on schedule
slack---the precise capability the problem demands. Adding $\tau$ and
$\beta$ to the observation restored the Markov property.

\paragraph{(iii) Exploration--commitment deadlock.} Run~A
(Table~\ref{tab:runs}): with a constant entropy bonus the policy remained
\emph{exactly uniform} for 100 epochs while still earning $-2470$ (uniform
random notches average out to a feasible pace). Sampled behavior masked the
defect; only the argmax (notch 0, timeout) and the entropy trace exposed
it. Linear annealing (Section~\ref{sec:mdp}) resolved the deadlock without
sacrificing early exploration: run~B improved the best reward by 147 units
and produced the first sharp, usable policy.

The 200-epoch forward policy (run~C) reaches \textbf{790.0\kwh{} in
6{,}417\,s} when sampled---83\,s inside the deadline. Linear interpolation
of the constant-notch frontier at 6{,}417\,s gives 790.8\kwh: the learned
policy sits on the frontier to within measurement noise
(Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff_fwd}). Against fixed references this is
\textbf{8.4\% less energy than flat-out driving} (notch 8, 862.4\kwh) and
\textbf{6.9\% less than the simulator's native driver} (848.2\kwh), while
arriving on schedule. The notch profile (Figure~\ref{fig:profile_fwd})
shows a low-notch base with selective bursts---consistent with the
shallow-top/steep-bottom structure of the frontier, which rewards spending
time at low notch and buying pace in short, efficient efforts.

Two caveats are part of the honest record. The policy \emph{matches} the
frontier; it does not yet move below it---on this route, with this
resistance model, sub-trip terrain modulation appears to offer little
energy beyond what constant-notch selection already captures, a finding
corroborated by the native varied-notch driver sitting \emph{above} the
frontier. Second, the deterministic argmax of run~C collapses to constant
notch~1 and times out; the sampled policy is the deployable artifact, and
the argmax pathology is analyzed next.

\subsection{Return trip and the lateness-pricing defect}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.8\linewidth]{fig_tradeoff_return.png}
\caption{Return trip: frontier, deadline, and RL outcomes. The 100-epoch
argmax (red diamond) lands exactly on constant notch 4, on schedule; the
200-epoch sampled policy (orange star) descends past the eco point into
cheap-but-late territory (169.6\kwh, 8.9\% late).}
\label{fig:tradeoff_ret}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.9\linewidth]{fig_profile_return.png}
\caption{Return-trip sampled policy: near-constant notch 1 with sparse
corrective bursts on the $-347$\,m net descent.}
\label{fig:profile_ret}
\end{figure}

The descending return trip is far cheaper everywhere
(Table~\ref{tab:frontier}); its eco target is constant notch~2
(242.2\kwh{} / 6{,}375\,s). At 100 epochs (run~D) the argmax is a clean,
on-schedule constant notch~4 (317.7\kwh)---correct side of the deadline,
two notches above the optimum, and still improving when the budget ended.
Doubling the budget (run~E) continued the descent \emph{past} the eco
point: the sampled policy settles at near-constant notch~1
(Figure~\ref{fig:profile_ret}), spending 169.6\kwh{} but arriving 576\,s
(8.9\%) late; the argmax is constant notch~1 at 18\% late
(Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff_ret}).

This is not an optimization failure---reward increased monotonically and
the policy is sharp---but a \emph{pricing} defect in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward}. Descending one notch from the eco point saves
$\sim$72\kwh{} ($216$ reward units at $w_E{=}3$) on the return trip, while
the pace penalty it incurs at $w_P{=}2$ is of comparable magnitude; the
optimum of the \emph{reward} therefore sits slightly below the deadline
pace even though the \emph{intended} optimum is on it. The same mechanism
explains every argmax pathology in Table~\ref{tab:runs}: the mode of the
policy settles at the energy floor of the reward-viable region, one notch
below schedule-feasible, while sampling noise supplies the missing pace.
The fix is a calibration, not a redesign: raise $w_P$ until the marginal
lateness cost strictly dominates the marginal one-notch energy saving on
both trips ($w_P \gtrsim 4$--$6$ by the arithmetic above), or impose the
deadline as a hard constraint (e.g.\ via a large terminal penalty scaled by
lateness \emph{and} a per-step pace term). We leave the retrained result to
the final version of this paper. \todo{run $w_P$ ablation and update}

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{What generalizes.} The four failure modes we document---data
artifacts that silently break simulator physics, observation normalization
drift, schedule rewards without clocks in the observation, and entropy
bonuses that overpower weak per-decision advantages---are not specific to
trains. Any simulator-in-the-loop RL project with long horizons and
hand-maintained input data is exposed to all four, and each has a cheap,
specific audit: integrate and plot derived physical profiles; histogram
each observation feature against its bounds; check reward terms for
dependence on state outside the observation; and track policy entropy
against $\ln |\mathcal{A}|$.

\paragraph{Energy accounting under force-balance violation.} NeTrainSim
charges energy from virtual tractive power $(ma + R)v$. When data errors
make $R$ exceed the adhesion limit, recorded power can exceed installed
power several-fold. After data repair the maximum per-second energy
(1.39\kwh) is consistent with installed power and transmission efficiency.
Simulation studies that aggregate energy over routes with unaudited grade
data inherit this risk.

\paragraph{Limitations.} (i)~Results are single-route, single-consist, and
in-simulator; no field validation is claimed. (ii)~The curvature unit in
the source data is unverified (footnote, Section~\ref{sec:simulator});
the curve resistance term is small under either plausible reading.
(iii)~The deterministic argmax is currently unusable on both trips; we
present sampled rollouts and have identified the calibration cause.
(iv)~Regenerative braking is excluded by design for the studied stock; on
the $-347$\,m return trip regeneration would change conclusions
substantially. (v)~The 15\,s action repeat that makes credit assignment
tractable also lower-bounds how finely the policy can modulate; whether
1--5\,s control with a hierarchical or reward-shaped scheme can move
\emph{below} the frontier remains open.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================

We built and audited an end-to-end pipeline that couples PPO to a modified
NeTrainSim with true per-second throttle control, repaired the route data
it depends on, and trained driving policies for both directions of the
Toshkent--Ho'jakent line. The forward-trip policy attains on-schedule
parity with the constant-notch Pareto frontier (790.0\kwh{} / 6{,}417\,s;
$-8.4\%$ vs.\ flat-out, $-6.9\%$ vs.\ the simulator's native driver), and
the complete diagnosis chain---data repair, observation audit, Markov
repair, entropy annealing, lateness pricing---is documented with
measurements at every step. The remaining gap between the learned mode and
the schedule-feasible optimum is a one-parameter reward calibration with a
known arithmetic, not an open-ended search. Beyond the specific line, the
study functions as a practical checklist for applying long-horizon RL
through imperfect simulators on imperfect data.

\paragraph{Future work.} (1)~Raise the lateness price ($w_P$) and report
on-schedule convergence on both trips; (2)~regeneration-enabled stock on
the descending direction; (3)~finer control intervals with hierarchical
policies to probe for below-frontier terrain modulation; (4)~robustness to
consist mass and timetable perturbations; (5)~field data calibration of
the GOST coefficients and the curvature unit.
